Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are brief, extremely bright flashes of gamma rays from distant galaxies.
They are the most luminous explosions known: for a few seconds a single burst can outshine every
other gamma-ray source in the sky. GRBs were discovered in the late 1960s by the Vela
satellites, which were built to watch for nuclear tests, and their origin remained a puzzle for
decades. In 1997 the BeppoSAX satellite made it possible to find fading X-ray and optical
"afterglows", which placed bursts in galaxies billions of light-years away.

Bursts are commonly split by duration, measured as T90, the time over which 90% of the gamma-ray
counts arrive:

- **Long GRBs** (longer than about 2 seconds) come from the collapse of rapidly rotating massive
  stars. Many are accompanied by a broad-lined Type Ic supernova.
- **Short GRBs** (shorter than about 2 seconds) are mostly produced by mergers of two neutron stars
  or a neutron star and a black hole. This was confirmed on 17 August 2017, when the
  gravitational-wave event GW170817 was followed 1.7 seconds later by GRB 170817A.

Some bursts break this simple rule; for example, a few long bursts have been linked to mergers.

In both cases a newborn black hole or rapidly spinning neutron star launches a narrow jet moving
at nearly the speed of light. The gamma rays come from inside the jet. When the jet slams into
surrounding gas it produces the afterglow, which fades over hours to weeks in X-rays, optical
light and radio.

Why they matter: GRBs are visible across most of the observable universe, so they probe the first
stars and galaxies. Their afterglow spectra reveal the gas in their host galaxies. Short bursts
are linked to the production of heavy elements such as gold in neutron-star mergers. GRBs also
test physics at energies no laboratory can reach.

A typical sequence for one burst: a wide-field gamma-ray instrument such as Fermi GBM, Swift BAT
or SVOM detects it and issues a notice within seconds; X-ray and optical telescopes search the
error region; an afterglow is found and localized to arcseconds; large telescopes take a spectrum
to measure the redshift, which gives the distance.
