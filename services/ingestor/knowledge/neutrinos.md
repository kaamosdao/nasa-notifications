title: High-energy astrophysical neutrinos

Neutrinos are nearly massless, electrically neutral particles that interact with matter only very
weakly. Trillions pass through each person every second without effect. This makes them hard to
detect, but also makes them unique messengers: they escape dense environments that trap light,
are not deflected by magnetic fields and are not absorbed over cosmic distances. A neutrino
therefore points straight back to where it was produced.

High-energy astrophysical neutrinos, with energies from teraelectronvolts (TeV) to
petaelectronvolts (PeV), are produced when cosmic-ray protons collide with matter or radiation
near powerful accelerators. Finding their sources answers a century-old question: where the
highest-energy cosmic rays come from.

Detectors look for the faint blue Cherenkov light emitted by charged particles that a neutrino
creates when it does interact. There are two main event shapes:

- **Tracks** — a muon travels up to kilometres through the detector. Direction is reconstructed to
  about a degree or better, so tracks are used for real-time alerts.
- **Cascades** — a compact shower of light. Energy is measured well, but direction only to about
  ten degrees.

Most detected neutrinos are background produced by cosmic rays hitting Earth's atmosphere.
Real-time alerts therefore report how likely an event is to be astrophysical rather than
atmospheric.

Key results so far come from IceCube:

- In 2013 it announced the discovery of a diffuse flux of astrophysical neutrinos.
- On 22 September 2017 the neutrino alert IceCube-170922A (about 290 TeV) arrived during a
  gamma-ray flare of the blazar TXS 0506+056. It was the first evidence for a specific source of
  high-energy neutrinos.
- In 2022 IceCube reported evidence of neutrino emission from the active galaxy NGC 1068.

Why alerts matter: a single neutrino is rarely conclusive on its own. Its value comes from quick
follow-up. Gamma-ray, X-ray and optical telescopes check its error region for a flaring blazar, a
supernova or a tidal disruption event that could have produced it.
