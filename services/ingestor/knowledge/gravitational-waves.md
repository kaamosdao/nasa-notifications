Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime produced when massive objects accelerate. Einstein's
general relativity predicted them in 1916, but they are so weak that a passing wave changes the
length of a 4-kilometre detector arm by a tiny fraction of the width of a proton. They were first
detected directly on 14 September 2015 (GW150914), when the two LIGO detectors recorded the merger
of two black holes about 1.3 billion light-years away. The discovery was awarded the 2017 Nobel
Prize in Physics.

Ground-based detectors hear the final seconds to minutes before two compact objects merge. The
detected sources are compact binary coalescences:

- **BBH** — two black holes. By far the most common detection; they usually emit no light.
- **BNS** — two neutron stars. Can produce a short gamma-ray burst and a kilonova, a glow powered
  by the radioactive decay of freshly made heavy elements.
- **NSBH** — a neutron star and a black hole. May produce light if the neutron star is torn apart
  before it falls in.

The landmark event is GW170817, a binary neutron star merger detected on 17 August 2017. It was
seen in gravitational waves, as the gamma-ray burst GRB 170817A, and as the kilonova AT2017gfo in
the galaxy NGC 4993, about 130 million light-years away. It showed that neutron-star mergers
produce heavy elements and that gravitational waves travel at the speed of light.

A gravitational-wave detector cannot point, so a sky position comes from comparing arrival times
and signal strengths across several detectors. The result is a probability sky map, usually
summarized as the area containing 90% of the probability. With two or three detectors this
area is typically hundreds to thousands of square degrees, which is why finding a counterpart
needs wide-field telescopes and a coordinated search.

Why they matter: gravitational waves measure the masses and spins of black holes directly, test
general relativity in the strongest gravity, provide an independent measurement of the expansion
rate of the universe, and — together with light — reveal where heavy elements come from.
